How to create an ideal Technical Specification?

A packaging design brief is not bureaucracy or a “checkbox document” — it’s the simplest way to save time, money, and nerves for everyone involved in the process.

  • Start with the reason for the project: be honest with yourself about why you’re doing this at all — has the market changed, has the design become outdated, has the product outgrown its old shell, or are you entering a new category?

  • Next comes the product itself: what it is, in what form, how it truly differs from competitors, and what must be preserved without compromise. Make sure to document what already exists — the brand name, website, brand book, previous packaging, POS materials, or, on the contrary, a complete blank slate. This immediately gives the designer a clear starting point.

  • The next step is brand character — not abstract “nice,” but specific traits: bold or caring, provocative or calm, premium or approachable. This is the language the packaging will use to speak from the shelf. Then define sales channels: retail, specialty stores, Instagram, gas stations, vending. Packaging for a supermarket and packaging for Instagram sales solve very different problems, and the designer needs to know this upfront.

  • Describe the consumer separately: who they are, how they choose, what they notice first, where they hesitate, and what they are willing to pay extra for. This directly shapes information hierarchy and visual emphasis.

  • After that, move to first-glance emotion: what should the packaging trigger in the first 2–3 seconds — appetite, trust, surprise, a sense of innovation, or calm confidence? To avoid explaining everything abstractly, add stylistic references — not as “do it like this,” but as mood and quality benchmarks.

  • Don’t forget about the packaging format and structure: doypack, box, tube, bucket — construction often dictates the design more strongly than the logo itself.

  • And the final, critically important block is information on the packaging: what must be on the front, what is secondary, which elements are legally required, and which meanings must not be lost under any circumstances.

When all of this is collected in one document, the designer no longer works blindly but as a partner who understands the task, context, and goal — which means the result is faster, more precise, and noticeably stronger.

And one last important note: if at some point you realize that gathering all this information is difficult — that’s completely normal. Not every company has the time, experience, or perspective to clearly define positioning, emotions, and a development vector.
